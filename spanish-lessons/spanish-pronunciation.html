A note on dialect This guide uses Latin American pronunciation as its reference (the norm across Peru, Ecuador, Mexico and most of the Americas): c/z before e, i sound like s (seseo), and ll sounds like y (yeísmo). Where mainland Spain differs, it's flagged.
Reading the IPA transcriptions Slashes / / show the phoneme (the abstract sound); brackets [ ] show a specific realization. A raised tick ˈ sits before the stressed syllable, and a dot . marks a syllable break — so /kanˈsjon/ is stressed on the second syllable. The softened symbols [β] [ð] [ɣ] are the relaxed, between-vowels versions of b/v, d, g. Terms like voiceless, bilabial, and plosive name the voicing, place, and manner of each sound; they're defined inline as they come up.
Lesson 01 · Acentuación

Stress & Accent

Every Spanish word has one syllable that gets the emphasis. Say gato and you naturally punch the first syllable: GA-to. Spanish is unusual in that its spelling tells you exactly where that punch goes. There are only two default patterns and one override — that's the whole system.

Stress in IPA In the International Phonetic Alphabet, primary stress is marked with a raised tick ˈ placed immediately before the stressed syllable, and dots . separate syllables. So gato is /ˈɡa.to/ — the tick tells you the first syllable carries the beat. Watch for that ˈ in the transcriptions below; it does the same job as the CAPS in the examples.
Rule 1 · llanas

1Ends in a vowel, -n, or -s → stress the second-to-last syllable

This is the most common Spanish word. The emphasis lands on the penultimate syllable (the one before the last).

ca-sa ha-bla-mos lu-nes jo-ven
IPA/ˈka.sa/ · /aˈβla.mos/ · /ˈlu.nes/ · /ˈxo.βen/ — the ˈ sits before the penultimate syllable in every case.
Rule 2 · agudas

2Ends in any other consonant → stress the last syllable

If a word ends in a consonant that is not -n or -s, the emphasis jumps to the final syllable. Notice this covers most infinitives (-ar, -er, -ir).

ha-blar ciu-dad es-pa-ñol re-loj
IPA/aˈβlaɾ/ · /sjuˈðað/ · /es.paˈɲol/ · /reˈlox/ — the tick lands on the final syllable.
Rule 3 · the tilde

3An accent mark (´) overrides everything — stress goes exactly there

This is the rule that makes reading Spanish easy: if you see a written accent, that is the stressed syllable, no exceptions. The accent appears precisely when a word breaks Rules 1 and 2, so the writing has to warn you.

can-ción ár-bol ca-fé lá-piz
IPA/kanˈsjon/ · /ˈaɾ.βol/ · /kaˈfe/ · /ˈla.pis/ — the written accent and the IPA tick point to the same syllable.
Why it's there canción ends in -n, so Rule 1 would predict CAN-cion. But we say can-CIÓN — it breaks the rule, so it needs a tilde. Same logic for árbol: it ends in a consonant (Rule 2 predicts the last syllable), but we stress the first, so the tilde flags it.
Rule 4 · esdrújulas

4Stress on the third-to-last syllable → ALWAYS written with an accent

When emphasis falls further back than the penultimate syllable, Spanish always marks it. So any word you see stressed on the antepenultimate syllable will carry a tilde — and any word with a tilde that far back is stressed there.

mú-si-ca te-lé-fo-no rá-pi-do pá-ja-ro
IPA/ˈmu.si.ka/ · /teˈle.fo.no/ · /ˈra.pi.ðo/ · /ˈpa.xa.ɾo/ — the tick sits on the antepenultimate (third-from-last) syllable.
Bonus · the diacritic accent Some tiny words wear an accent only to tell twins apart — the stress and sound are identical: tú (you) vs tu (your), él (he) vs el (the), sí (yes) vs si (if), más (more) vs mas (but). You'll meet these constantly; just notice the mark carries meaning, not extra emphasis.

Lesson 02 · Consonantes

Consonants

Spanish consonants are consistent — but several change sound depending on the letters around them. The trick isn't memorizing each word; it's learning the small set of triggers: which vowel follows, whether a letter starts the word, and what sits next door.

The full inventory, by articulation

Every consonant sound is defined by three things: voicing (are the vocal folds vibrating?), place (where in the mouth the airflow is obstructed), and manner (how it's obstructed). Here is the Latin American consonant system.

Rule 1 · soft vs hard

1C and G soften before E and I

The following vowel decides everything.

Letterbefore A, O, Ubefore E, I
chard /k/ — casa, comosoft /s/ — cena, cine
ghard /g/ — gato, gotathroaty /x/ — gente, gigante
casa cena gato gente
IPA/ˈka.sa/ · /ˈse.na/ · /ˈɡa.to/ · /ˈxen.te/
Articulation Hard c = [k] voiceless velar plosive; soft c = [s] voiceless alveolar fricative (Spain: [θ] dental). Hard g = [ɡ] voiced velar plosive; soft g = [x] voiceless velar fricative. Notice the softening changes the manner from a full stop to a fricative — and, for g, drops the voicing too.
Keeping a sound hard To keep the hard sound before e/i, Spanish inserts a silent u: guerra, guitarra (hard g) and uses qu for hard c: queso, quién. If that u must actually be pronounced, it gets two dots — pingüino, vergüenza.
Rule 2 · silent letters

2H is always silent · U is silent in que/qui/gue/gui

The letter h is never pronounced. And the u in those four combinations is just a spelling device — you don't say it.

hola ahora queso guitarra
IPA/ˈo.la/ · /aˈo.ɾa/ · /ˈke.so/ · /ɡiˈta.ra/ — no segment for h, and no /w/ for the silent u.
Articulation h maps to no phoneme at all (∅) — Spanish simply has no /h/ in these words. The digraphs qu and gu(before e/i) are pure spelling: they encode a single [k] or [ɡ] with no glide.
Rule 3 · tap vs trill

3Single r taps · rr (and initial r) trills

A single r between vowels is a quick tap — the same flap as the tt in the American English "butter." A double rr, or an r at the start of a word or after n, l, s, is a rolled trill.

pero (tap) perro (trill) rojo (trill) Enrique (trill)
IPA/ˈpe.ɾo/ (tap) · /ˈpe.ro/ (trill) · /ˈro.xo/ · /enˈri.ke/
Articulation Both are voiced alveolar sounds; only the manner differs. [ɾ] is a tap — one quick strike of the tongue. [r] is a trill — the tongue vibrates in several rapid strikes. This is the single feature that separates pero from perro.
This one is a minimal pairThe tap/trill difference changes meaning: pero = "but," perro = "dog." Worth drilling.
Rule 4 · position softening

4B/V and D soften between vowels

Two things surprise English speakers here. First, b and v sound identical — there is no English "v" sound in Spanish. Second, these stops relax when surrounded by vowels:

Letterat a start / after n, m, lbetween vowels
b, vfull stop /b/ — vino, hombresoft /β/ lips barely touch — uva, haber
dfull stop /d/ — dónde, faldasoft /ð/ like "th" in this — nada, usted
vino uva dónde nada
IPA/ˈbi.no/ · /ˈu.βa/ · /ˈdon.de/ · /ˈna.ða/ — same phoneme, two realizations each.
Articulation — allophones in complementary distribution The phonemes /b/ (voiced bilabial) and /d/ (voiced dental) each surface two ways depending on position. After a pause or a nasal (and, for /d/, after l) they are full plosives [b], [d̪]. Everywhere else — crucially between vowels — they weaken to approximants [β], [ð]: the articulators come close but never fully close, so the airflow is never stopped. The place and voicing stay constant; only the manner relaxes. (The same pattern gives /ɡ/ → [ɣ].)

5Letters that always sound the same: J · Ñ · LL · Z

Articulation j = [x] voiceless velar fricative. ñ = [ɲ] voiced palatal nasal. ll (yeísmo) = [ʝ] voiced palatal approximant. z = [s] voiceless alveolar fricative in Latin America. Each of these maps to one stable sound regardless of surroundings.

Lesson 03 · Vocales

Vowels

Spanish has five vowels and five sounds — and they never change. This is genuinely good news: unlike English, where "a" is different in cat, cake, car, and about, the Spanish a is always the same clean sound, wherever it sits. The main job for English speakers is to stop doing three things English does automatically.

a = "ah" e = "eh" i = "ee" o = "oh" u = "oo"

The five vowels, by articulation

Vowels don't obstruct airflow, so instead of place and manner they're described by tongue height (how open the mouth is), backness (front vs back of the mouth), and lip rounding. All five Spanish vowels are voiced and tense.

IPAHeightBacknessRoundingExample
iclose (high)frontunroundedsí
emidfrontunroundedmesa
aopen (low)centralunroundedcasa
omidbackroundedno
uclose (high)backroundedtú
The two glides In diphthongs, the weak vowels i and u become glides (non-syllabic approximants): i → [j] (palatal, as in tierra /ˈtje.ra/) and u → [w] (labial-velar, as in cuento /ˈkwen.to/). A glide is the same tongue position as the vowel, but passed through quickly rather than held.
Rule 1 · pure vowels

1Keep each vowel pure — no gliding

English vowels slide: "no" is really "no-oo" (o→w) and "day" is "day-ee" (e→y). Spanish vowels are single, steady sounds that don't move. Lock your mouth in place and hold it.

WordEnglish habit (avoid)Spanish target
no"noh-oo"clean "no"
mesa"may-sa""meh-sa"
su"soo-oo"clean "su"
no mesa café
IPA/no/ · /ˈme.sa/ · /kaˈfe/ — each vowel is a single steady target: [e], not the English gliding [eɪ].
Rule 2 · no reduction

2Unstressed vowels stay full — no "uh"

In English, unstressed vowels collapse into a lazy "uh" (schwa): banana → "buh-NAN-uh." Spanish never does this. Every vowel keeps its full, clear quality whether it's stressed or not.

WordEnglish reduction (avoid)Spanish target
banana"buh-NAN-uh""ba-NA-na" (three clear a's)
problema"pruh-BLEM-uh""pro-BLE-ma"
banana problema señorita
IPA/baˈna.na/ · /proˈβle.ma/ · /se.ɲoˈɾi.ta/ — full [a e i o] in every syllable, never the reduced [ə] (schwa).
Rule 3 · diphthongs

3Weak + strong vowel = one syllable (a diphthong)

Now the "surrounding sounds" part. The vowels split into two groups:

The key distinction Strong (open): A, E, O  ·  Weak (closed): I, U

When a weak vowel (i, u) touches a strong one, they glide together into a single syllable. The weak vowel becomes a quick /y/ or /w/ slide.

baile = bai-le tierra = tie-rra cuento = cuen-to ciudad = ciu-dad
IPA/ˈbaj.le/ · /ˈtje.ra/ · /ˈkwen.to/ · /sjuˈðað/ — the weak vowel surfaces as a glide [j] or [w], sharing one syllable with its neighbor.
Rule 4 · hiatus

4Two strong vowels split apart (hiatus) — and an accent can force a split

When two strong vowels meet, they belong to separate syllables — you pronounce each fully. And a written accent on a weak vowel breaks up what would have been a diphthong, pulling the two vowels into separate syllables.

WordSplitWhy
leerle-ertwo strong vowels (e + e)
poetapo-e-tatwo strong vowels (o + e)

IPA/leˈeɾ/ · /poˈe.ta/ · /ˈdi.a/ · /paˈis/ — two full syllabic vowels, no glide; the dot marks the break.
Tie-in with Lesson 1Diphthongs vs. hiatus decide how many syllables a word has — which decides which syllable the stress rules point at. The three systems connect.
