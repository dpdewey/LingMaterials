Language, in the brain,
wherever it happens.

We use functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to study how people acquire and process language — in the lab, in classrooms, at live storytelling events, and in the Ecuadorian Amazon. Because fNIRS is portable and tolerant of movement, we can image the brain where language is actually used.

What we study

four active lines of work See the papers

Ideophones and multisensory meaning

Neural evidence for how sound-symbolic words carry sensory meaning in Japanese, Chinese, and Pastaza Kichwa — including the first fNIRS data collected in the Ecuadorian Amazon.

Social interaction and language learning

How learning with another person changes what the brain does with a new language, and whether social connectedness in a classroom leaves a measurable neural trace.

Hyperscanning and neural coupling

Simultaneous imaging of speakers and listeners — up to five people at once — during live public storytelling, to study how brains synchronize and what that has to do with belonging.

Emotion, stress, and assessment

Emotion-word processing in bilinguals, pre-exam relaxation and its effect on neural activation and test performance, and cognitive load during oral proficiency interviews.
