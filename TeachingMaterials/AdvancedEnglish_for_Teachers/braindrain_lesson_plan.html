PHASE 1 — WARM-UP
5 MIN · TTT: ~2 MIN

Procedure

  1. Distribute or display the Teach for Success Brain Drain Survey. Give participants 2 minutes to read silently — not to complete it, just to scan it and notice which questions feel most personal.
  2. Ask: "Which question hit closest to home? Did any question make you want to answer immediately — or made you hesitate?" (1 min think time; 1 min open share — take 2–3 brief responses with no commentary.)
  3. Frame the lesson: "Today the survey is our starting point. We're going to talk about one of the most important decisions your students face — and maybe one that shaped your own life. We'll also build the English vocabulary you need to have this conversation at a high level."
The question "should I stay or should I go?" is personal for almost everyone in the room — they either stayed, came back, or have a sibling or child who left or made a hard choice to stay. I simply distribute the survey and let the room's dynamic do much of the work. The things I watch for: who pauses longest on some of the questions or who has the strongest opinions one way or the other on any given idea.   
The warm-up follows the same structure as previous Teach for Success lessons: the survey is used as an activator, not a data instrument. By asking participants to read before they speak, the teacher lowers the entry threshold — everyone has something to look at, which prevents the awkward silence that can accompany topics that are difficult to discuss due to the emotional content or linguistic demands.
Teacher talk: ~2 minutes
PHASE 2 — VOCABULARY INPUT
10 MIN · TTT: ~4 MIN

Refer participants to Vocabulary Set 1 and Set 2 on the handout.

Set 1 — Economic Migration & Development Vocabulary (8 items)

For each item: pronounce → one example sentence from a Peruvian context → invite a second example or synonym from participants. Move quickly; this is exchange, not lecture.

WORD / PHRASEMEANING IN CONTEXTEXAMPLE
brain drain (n)the emigration of educated or skilled workers from a region, leaving it less developed"Ayacucho has experienced significant brain drain as graduates move to Lima."
urban migration (n)the movement of people from rural to urban areas in search of better opportunities"Urban migration has accelerated since the university opened in our province."
community investment (n)the use of money, time, or skills to develop and improve a local community"Young doctors who return home are a form of community investment."
entrepreneurship (n)the activity of setting up new businesses, often while taking on financial risk"Rural entrepreneurship can create jobs without requiring people to leave."
to retain (v)to keep someone or something rather than losing them to another place"How can rural provinces retain their most talented graduates?"
economic opportunity (n)the chance to earn a living wage, advance professionally, or build wealth"Students leave because economic opportunities in Lima are far greater."
remittances (n)money sent home by workers living in another city or country"Remittances from Lima help many rural families survive."
local talent pipeline (n)a system that develops skilled people within a community and keeps them there"Our university could become a local talent pipeline if salaries were competitive."

Set 2 — Expressing Values, Tension, and Identity (9 items)

These are the words participants need to express the emotional and ethical dimensions of this topic at B2–C1 level.

WORDMEANINGUSE IN CONTEXT
torn (adj)unable to choose between two things you value equally"Many graduates feel torn between their career and their family obligations."
rooted (adj)having a strong connection to a place, culture, or community"I feel deeply rooted here — leaving would feel like losing part of myself."
obligated (adj)feeling a strong duty or responsibility to do something"Some students feel obligated to return and repay their community."
ambitious (adj)having a strong desire to succeed, achieve, or do more"Being ambitious isn't incompatible with staying — it depends on the opportunities available."
resentful (adj)feeling bitterness toward a person or situation that seems unfair"Those who stayed can feel resentful watching peers advance in Lima."
pragmatic (adj)dealing with problems in a practical, realistic way rather than an idealistic one"A pragmatic approach means looking at what salaries are actually available here."
conflicted (adj)experiencing two opposing feelings or values at the same time"I felt genuinely conflicted when I got the job offer in Arequipa."
indebted (adj)feeling that you owe something to a person or community"Many scholarship recipients feel indebted to their home province."
underutilized (adj)not being used to full potential or capacity"Talented young people feel underutilized in areas with few professional opportunities."
I often  write words like  brain drain, torn, and rooted on the board immediately, because these three words tend to unlock conversations that couldn't happen before. "Rooted" in particular is often new to B2 learners but immediately resonates.
The Set 2 words are all gradable adjectives describing internal states. This is pedagogically deliberate: they provide learners with the emotional vocabulary to move from reporting facts ("many people leave") to expressing personal experience ("I felt conflicted because…"). Without this register, discussion at B2–C1 (Advanced Level) stays at the surface.
Teacher talk: ~4 minutes
PHASE 3 — INTERACTION 1: PAIR DISCUSSION
8 MIN · TTT: ~1 MIN

Procedure

  1. Put participants in pairs, ideally mixed by age or years at the institution. Refer them to Section A on the handout.
  2. They choose two questions to discuss for 6 minutes. Circulate and listen only — note language for discussion in the clinic. Be careful to not join, correct, or comment much--this is student talk time. You are only providing a bit of scaffolding here and there.
  3. Bring the class together. Have them share one sentence from one pair as time allows. Move immediately to the Language Clinic.

Section A Questions (on the handout)

  1. Did you face a choice between staying in your hometown and moving to a larger city? What factors most influenced your decision — or what factors would influence it if you had to choose now?
  2. Think of a former student who left the region. Think of one who stayed. Who do you think made the better choice? Is it fair to compare them?
  3. Is there a difference between leaving for economic reasons and leaving for personal ambitions? Does the reason change how you feel about it?
  4. What would need to change in your province for a talented twenty-five-year-old to choose to stay — not out of obligation, but because it genuinely seemed like the best option?
"Choose any two. You don't have to agree. You have 6 minutes — talk as much as you like."
Question 2 is the one I think will most reliably generate animated discussion with teacher groups. The comparison of two former students is concrete and real — it avoids the abstraction of "young people in general." I often hear people say "I actually thought of someone specific immediately." If a pair finishes their two questions early, I nudge them toward Question 4, which tends to produce more forward-looking, solution-oriented language.
The pair-work format is essential for this topic because brain drain is emotionally loaded. Group or whole-class formats can create pressure to perform a socially acceptable position or to produce face-saving language (socially acceptable that demonstrates linguistic strengths). Pairs allow people to be more honest about ambivalence or personal regret — which produces richer language than rehearsed opinion. They also allow for more mistakes and negotiation of meaning. The latter is critical for language development.
Teacher talk: ~1 minute
PHASE 4 — LANGUAGE CLINIC 1
5 MIN · TTT: ~3 MIN

Procedure

  1. Open: "Any grammar or vocabulary questions from your pair discussion? Anything you wanted to say but couldn't find the English for?"
  2. Share 1–2 language patterns you noticed while circulating. No names. Rephrase into a target form and write it on the board.
  3. Ask participants to produce a second example sentence using the target form.

Anticipated Language Needs

LIKELY LEARNER FORMTARGET FORM
"He decided to go because he wanted earn more money.""He left in order to / so that he could earn a better wage."
"Many peoples go to Lima.""Large numbers of / A significant proportion of graduates migrate to Lima."
"I don't think it's good that young people leave.""I'm skeptical that this trend benefits the region in the long term."
"She came back because she felt she must.""She returned because she felt obligated / indebted to her community."
"The town is missing important persons.""The community is losing its most skilled / educated residents to urban migration."
The first Language Clinic targets two common weaknesses at B2: insufficient lexical precision (using simple words when more precise ones are available) and the absence of cohesive devices that show logical relationship (because → in order to; I think → I'm skeptical that). The teacher's role is to provide the form cleanly without correcting the person who produced it.
Teacher talk: ~3 minutes
PHASE 5 — INTERACTION 2: PERSPECTIVE TASK (JIGSAW)
12 MIN · TTT: ~2 MIN

Setup — Argument Groups

Divide into two groups. Group A receives the Case for Staying panel on the handout. Group B receives the Case for Leaving panel. Give groups 3 minutes to read and identify the 3 strongest arguments on their card.

Note: assign groups strategically — if you know who has personally left and returned, consider putting them in the opposing group for maximum productive tension.

Expert Pairs — Structured Argument Exchange

Reform into A-B pairs. Each person must advocate for their assigned position (even if they personally disagree) for 4 minutes. Then drop the role and discuss openly: "Now, what do you actually think?" (2 min)

"For the first four minutes: argue the position on your card as strongly as you can, even if it's not your own view. Then we'll drop the roles."

Perspective Reference — For Handout Panels

GROUP A — THE CASE FOR STAYING
  • Rural communities lose their best talent just when they need it most
  • Universities, schools, and clinics in rural areas desperately need qualified staff
  • Lima is already overcrowded; internal migration creates urban poverty, not just rural poverty
  • Entrepreneurship and remote work now make it possible to build a career without leaving
  • Social capital — family networks, trust, community relationships — is worth more than a higher salary
  • Who will teach the next generation if all the teachers leave?
  • Cultural identity and ancestral knowledge are lost when educated youth become urban
GROUP B — THE CASE FOR LEAVING
  • Professional development, specialist training, and career advancement rarely exist in rural areas
  • Salaries in Lima can be 3–4× higher for the same work
  • Young people have the right to pursue the best opportunities available, regardless of geography
  • Migrants send remittances home — often more economic impact than staying on a low local salary
  • Exposure to urban life, education systems, and networks creates leaders who can later return with more
  • Staying in a limited environment can produce frustration, resentment, and underperformance
  • The brain drain problem is the government's responsibility to fix, not the individual's burden to carry
The role-play element is what makes Phase 5 work. When I simply ask "do you think people should stay or leave?" I get polite, uncommitted answers. When I say "you are now arguing the position on your card as if you have a personal stake--someone close involved, for example. One might say, "Make arguments as if your life depends on it" or something like that. The forced advocacy reveals the arguments people know but don't usually say out loud. The "now drop it — what do you actually think?" moment at the end is often the most honest conversation of the lesson.
Assigning positions — rather than letting learners choose — serves two pedagogical purposes. First, it exposes learners to the full range of arguments on both sides, which deepens their understanding and vocabulary. Second, it protects participants from social pressure: if your position was assigned, you are not personally judged for it. This is especially valuable on emotionally loaded topics where some participants may feel their own choices are implicitly under scrutiny.
Teacher talk: ~2 minutes
PHASE 6 — INTERACTION 3: LIKERT DEBATE
8 MIN · TTT: ~2 MIN

Procedure — Opinion Continuum

  1. Read a statement from Section C. Participants show their position on a 1–5 scale (fingers, paper, or physical position in the room--maybe the raise hand high, mid, low, or point down for level of agrement).
  2. Call on one person from each extreme: "One sentence — why did you choose that number?" Open briefly to the floor.
  3. Repeat with 3–4 statements. Move quickly — this is temperature-taking at this point, not debate.

Section C Statements (select 3–4)

  1. "Young people who leave their rural community to work in Lima are, in some sense, abandoning the people who need them most."
  2. "It is the government's responsibility, not the individual's, to make rural areas attractive enough to retain talent."
  3. "Universities in rural regions should focus more on preparing students to build businesses locally than to compete for jobs in national corporations."
  4. "A person who studied with a scholarship from a rural province has a moral obligation to return and contribute to that community."
  5. "Brain drain is actually good for rural communities in the long run, because migrants return with more skills, capital, and ambition than they left with."
  6. "Teachers and professors who stay in rural areas are making a greater social contribution than those who pursue more prestigious careers in cities."
Statement 6 is the one I save for last, because it is the most personally relevant to the room and because I like to complement teachers for making a difference. Participants are the ones who stayed. Asking them whether they believe they made a greater social contribution than those who left — and asking them to rate it from 1 to 5 — can produce visible emotion and the most honest responses of the lesson. I find myself looking forward to this moment every time.
Statements 1 and 4 both use morally loaded language ("abandoning," "moral obligation") deliberately. At B2–C1, learners need practice recognizing and responding to loaded language — agreeing or disagreeing with it, softening or strengthening it. This is a high-frequency skill in academic and professional English that is rarely taught directly.
Teacher talk: ~2 minutes
PHASE 7 — LANGUAGE CLINIC 2
4 MIN · TTT: ~2 MIN

Procedure

  1. Ask: "Did you find any words from Set 2 that you ended up using? Which ones felt natural? Which felt forced?"
  2. Ask: "Was there anything you wanted to say in the debate — a nuance, a qualification — that you couldn't express in English?"
  3. Address 1–2 items. Write any new target forms on the board. Close quickly.
The second Language Clinic is explicitly meta-linguistic: it asks learners to reflect on their own language use and any gaps between what they wanted to say and what they were able to say (called 'noticing the gap'. It also allows them to check/comfirm that they used language in appropriate ways (called 'confirmation check'). These are higher-order skills that contributes to long-term acquisition. Asking "which words felt natural?" also validates the vocabulary input — learners hear that some words will feel immediately usable and others will take time to become active.
Teacher talk: ~2 minutes
PHASE 8 — WRAP-UP & REFLECTION
3 MIN · TTT: ~2 MIN

Procedure

  1. One closing question — small and concrete:
    "One word from today's vocabulary that you'll think about after you leave — what is it? Just say it aloud. No explanation needed."
  2. Go around the room once. No comments. Let each word stand alone.
  3. Distribute or display the handout QR. Invite participants to fully complete the survey if they haven't already.
Some words I anticipating at the close of this lesson: rooted, torn, conflicted, obligated, underutilized. I find that students in situations such as this tend to choose not just the most sophisticated vocabulary. Instead  they tend to choose the words that describe exactly how they feel or how they think their students feel. The emotional component is strongly evident. That alignment between language and lived experience is, to me, what advanced ESL learning actually looks like. Students need to learn to discuss that which means the most to them..    
Ending on learner voices — one word each, no commentary — is a structural inversion of the traditional lesson close (teacher summarizes, teacher evaluates). It signals that the lesson's content was the participants' experience, and that the teacher's job was to give them better English for expressing what they already know.
Teacher talk: ~2 minutes
